# Camellia formosensis

## Taxonomic Notes

<!-- History of classification. Note any reclassifications or disputes. -->
<!-- E.g. Laitou and Shueijing populations misclassified across Lai 2001, Hu 2014, Lee 2017 -->

**New combination proposed in this paper:** *Camellia formosensis* (Masamune et Suzuki) M. H. Su, C. F. Hsieh et C. H. Tsou, comb. nov. (台灣山茶).

**Synonymy / taxonomic history, per Su et al. (2009):**
- **Basionym:** *Thea formosensis* Masamune et Suzuki (in Suzuki, S. 1937. *Taiwan Zyumoku Benran*, Taihoku (Taipei), p. 262) — the first valid publication of the taxon, but no specimens were cited in that original publication.
- **Neotype** (designated by Su et al. 2009, since the 1937 publication cited no specimens): Formosa (Taiwan), Taityu (Nantou Co.), Gyoti (Yuchih), 8 Nov 1935, flowering, S. Suzuki s.n. (TAI no. 218078).
- Because the genus *Thea* had already been treated as a synonym of *Camellia* by Sweet (1818), and this is accepted under Article 13.5 of the International Code of Botanical Nomenclature (Vienna Code; McNeill et al. 2006), the authors here formally combine *Thea formosensis* into *Camellia* as *C. formosensis*.
- **1950:** Kitamura published *Camellia sinensis* (L.) O. Kuntze forma *formosensis* Kitamura (*Acta Phytotax. Geobot.* 14: 59), citing a holotype (Japan, Kanaya Tea Research Station, a plant introduced from Taiwan, collected 21 Nov 1949 by S. Kitamura, holotype at KYO) — without citing the earlier *T. formosensis* publication. Su et al. (2009) find this holotype displays the same traits as *T. formosensis* and therefore treat *C. sinensis* forma *formosensis* as a synonym of the basionym. Also referenced under this forma-level name by Hsieh, Yang & Lin (1996, *Flora of Taiwan*, 2nd ed., Vol. 2: 662–693).
- Also historically named/referenced as *Camellia sinensis* subsp. *buisanensis* (Sasaki) Lu & Yang (Lu, S.Y. and Yang, Y.P. 1987. "The wild tea of Taiwan." *Quart. J. Chin. For.* 20: 101–107).
- **Ming (2000)** merged the taxon entirely into *Camellia sinensis* var. *sinensis* (Ming, T.L. 2000. *Monograph of the Genus Camellia*, Yunnan Science and Technology Press, Kunming, p. 130).
- The paper states this long taxonomic uncertainty "mostly stems from the high morphological similarities existing among these three taxa" (Taiwanese wild tea, *C. sinensis* var. *sinensis*, *C. sinensis* var. *assamica*).
- A prior morphological study by the same authors (Su et al. 2007, *Taiwania* 52: 70–83) had already shown the Taiwanese wild tea could be well differentiated from the other two taxa by its glabrous ovaries and winter buds (versus pubescent in the cultivated taxa).
- This 2009 paper's RPB2 sequence-based molecular evidence (see Molecular Evidence below) is presented as the confirming evidence for elevating the taxon to full species rank.

## Distribution

<!-- Which regions / counties / elevations is this species documented in? -->
<!-- Link to Region notes. -->

Endemic to Taiwan; distributed in the central, southern, and southeastern parts of the island, at elevations of **800–1,800 m**. Documented (per the formal taxonomic treatment) in **Nantou, Yunlin, Chiayi, Kaohsiung, Pingtung, and Taitung Counties**.

The molecular sampling in this study defined three geographically separated populations:
- **Nantou population** — e.g. Meiyuanshan, Derhuasia, Fenhuanshan, Yuchih, Yuchih Branch of the Tea Research and Extension Station, Luku, Shuili-to-Yuchih.
- **South population** — spanning Chiayi, Kaohsiung, and Pingtung Counties, e.g. Sitin (Chiayi), Yuyuoshan and Sasi Logging Road (Kaohsiung), Jenlishan and Wuweishan (Pingtung).
- **East population** — Taitung County, at Yungkangshan.

Distribution/sampling sites (Figure 1) were based on herbarium specimens held at TAI, TAIF, HAST, and TNM.

Additional specimen localities listed in the formal taxonomic treatment: **Nantou Co.** — Luku, Meiyuenshan, Shuili to Yuchih, Yuchih, Yuchih Branch (Tea Research and Extension Station); **Yunlin Co.** — Kulinjiao; **Chiayi Co.** — Kagi, Sitin; **Kaohsiung Co.** — Kakuhozan, Liukui, Nanfong Logging Road, Nanfongshan, Shanping, Tona; **Pingtung Co.** — Akohuzi, Chiupaoshan, Jenlishan, Paiwan, Sasi Logging Road, Wuweishan; **Taitung Co.** — Yungkangshan.

## Conservation Status

<!-- Is it designated? Proposed for designation? At risk? -->

No formal conservation designation (e.g. IUCN category) is stated in this paper. The paper does explicitly note that wild populations of Taiwanese wild tea "have drastically declined due to human disturbance" and that "conservation of the endemic tea is, therefore, urgently required," citing the role of taxonomy in species conservation (Mace, G.M. 2004. *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. Lond. B.* 359: 711–719) as the motivation for resolving the taxon's classification in the first place.

## Molecular Evidence

<!-- ITS2 barcoding, SSR, plastome — what molecular studies confirm this taxon? -->

**Gene region:** Introns 12–16 and intron 23 of the nuclear **RPB2** gene (single-copy gene encoding the second-largest subunit of RNA polymerase II; Denton et al. 1998).

**Sampling (35 OTUs total, Table 1):**
- 13 samples of Taiwanese wild tea (11 wild-collected; 2 from the tea germplasm garden at the Yuchih Branch, Taiwan Tea Experiment Station)
- 8 samples of *C. sinensis* var. *sinensis*
- 11 samples of *C. sinensis* var. *assamica*
- 3 outgroup species from other sections: *C. furfuracea* (sect. Furfuracea), *C. transarisanensis* (sect. Theopsis), *C. brevistyla* (sect. Paracamellia)
- All voucher specimens deposited at TAI or HAST; GenBank accession numbers listed per sample for both intron regions.

**Methods:**
- DNA extraction from dried leaf tissue, following Vijayan and Tsou (2008).
- PCR: 50 µL reactions (100 ng DNA, 0.2–0.6 µM each primer, 200 µM dNTP, 0.5 U Taq polymerase, 1X buffer, Viogene Inc.); thermal profile 95 °C 5 min, then 35 cycles of 95 °C 1 min / 58–63 °C 1 min 20 s / 72 °C 1 min 20 s, final extension 72 °C 7 min.
- Products purified with QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen); sequenced on an ABI PRISM 3700 DNA Sequencer.
- Primers (Table 2): introns 12–16 via C-1-11F/C-1-16R (Xiao 2001) and C-1-11F2/C-1-16R2 (newly designed for this study); intron 23 via C-1-10AF/C-1-11R (Xiao 2001) and C-1-10AF1/C-1-11R1 (newly designed for this study).
- Alignment with CLUSTAL_X v1.83 (Thompson et al. 1997).
- Phylogenetic analysis: Maximum Parsimony (branch-and-bound search, strict consensus) and Neighbor Joining (uncorrected "p" distance), both in PAUP v0.4b10 (Swofford 2001); indels treated as single base changes (fifth base in MP; missing data in NJ); branch support from 1000 bootstrap replicates; genetic distances calculated with the uncorrected "p" index.

**Sequence data:** Introns 12–16 = 954 bp in all samples, no indels. Intron 23 = 992–1001 bp, except two *C. sinensis* var. *assamica* samples (A-Thai-2, A-Ind-3) carrying a 284 bp insertion (treated as a single mutation event). Final aligned matrix = 1923 bp across 35 OTUs; 166 bp variable, 75 bp parsimony-informative.

**Results:**
- MP analysis (branch-and-bound) found 1896 equally parsimonious trees; strict consensus tree = 233 steps; consistency index (CI) = 0.92, homoplasy index (HI) = 0.25 (low homoplasy). MP and NJ trees shared the same topology (branch-support values differed slightly).
- **All 13 *C. formosensis* samples formed a single, well-separated, monophyletic clade** with high bootstrap support (**92% MP, 99% NJ**).
- Within that clade, two significantly supported subclades: the **South population** on its own, and the **Nantou + East populations** together in a second subclade.
- By contrast, the 19 combined samples of *C. sinensis* var. *assamica* and var. *sinensis* were **polytomous** (unresolved) in the cladogram — attributed to their long history of cultivation and hybridization within the tea industry, which would produce expected introgression between the two.

**Genetic distances (uncorrected "p"; Table 3):**
- *C. formosensis* vs. *C. sinensis* var. *assamica* = 0.0092 ± 0.0022
- *C. formosensis* vs. *C. sinensis* var. *sinensis* = 0.0079 ± 0.0026
- *C. sinensis* var. *sinensis* vs. var. *assamica* = 0.0043 ± 0.0023 (roughly half of either *C. formosensis*-to-cultivar distance)
- Among the three *C. formosensis* populations: Nantou–East = 0.0016 ± 0.0006; South–Nantou = 0.0038 ± 0.0002; South–East = 0.0033 ± 0.0002 — all smaller than the 0.0043 sinensis–assamica interspecific distance.
- The paper compares its *C. formosensis*-to-cultivar distances (0.0092, 0.0079) to the average pairwise RPB2 distance previously reported among species of section Thea as a whole — 0.0087 ± 0.0054 (Xiao 2001; Xiao and Parks 2003) — and finds them equivalent, i.e. *C. formosensis* sits at a genetic distance from the cultivated taxa that is typical of *inter-species* distances within the section, supporting species rank.

**Independent corroboration:** Vijayan et al. (2009, in press at the time of writing) found a similar species-level separation using nrITS sequence analysis, which the authors cite as independent support for the same conclusion.

## Source Conflicts

<!-- Taxonomic disputes between sources — document here with provenance chain. -->

- **Kitamura (1950)** treated the taxon at forma rank, as *C. sinensis* forma *formosensis*, without citing the 1937 basionym publication. Su et al. (2009) resolve this by treating it as a synonym of *Thea formosensis*.
- **Ming (2000)**, in the *Monograph of the Genus Camellia*, merged the taxon entirely into *C. sinensis* var. *sinensis* — the most recent pre-2009 treatment superseded by this paper's species-level combination.
- **Lai et al. (2001)** (RAPD/ISSR fingerprinting of tea cultivars, including the Taiwanese wild tea) found all Taiwanese wild tea samples clustering together in their phylogenetic trees — consistent with distinctness, but per Su et al. (2009) that study's sampling was too limited (too few samples, and no plants from the eastern population) to resolve taxonomic rank on its own.
- **Wachira et al. (2001)** (RAPD/AFLP) reached a similar clustering conclusion but included only two Taiwanese wild tea samples.
- **Xiao (2001) and Xiao and Parks (2003)** used RPB2 sequences to study *Camellia* phylogeny broadly and provided insight into section-level relationships, but included **no** Taiwanese wild tea material at all — a gap this 2009 study explicitly set out to fill using the same DNA segments with much more extensive sampling.
- This paper's own morphology-vs-molecular note: an earlier phenetic/morphological analysis (Su et al. 2007) placed the Nantou population as closer to the South population than to the East population, whereas this paper's molecular (RPB2) analysis instead groups Nantou with East, separate from South. The authors attribute this incongruence to possible convergent morphological adaptation — specifically, that the leaf/winter-bud pubescence distinguishing the East population may be an adaptive response to the strong northeast winter monsoon winds that reach Taitung (where the East population is located) but diminish after passing the Central Mountain Range, rather than a phylogenetically informative trait.

## Open Questions

The paper explicitly frames one point as an assumption rather than a settled conclusion: whether the leaf/winter-bud pubescence found in the East population is truly an adaptive trait evolved in response to windy monsoon conditions (as opposed to some other cause of the morphology-vs-molecular incongruence between this study and the earlier Su et al. 2007 phenetic analysis). The authors state this "could be the main factor," framed as their own hypothesis, not as demonstrated fact.
