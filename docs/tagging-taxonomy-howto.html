Building a Literature Tagging Taxonomy

Step 1 of Knowledge Management — Tea Heritage Knowledge Graph Project

Author

KL

Published

June 28, 2026

Why this document exists

Before extracting data from sources (GBIF, NCBI, TRES bulletins), the literature you read needs a consistent, controlled set of tags in Obsidian/Zotero. Without this, you will end up with synonyms scattered across notes (e.g. #formosensis, #C.formosensis, #taiwan-wild-tea all meaning the same thing), and you will have to redo the tagging later — which costs more time than doing it right now.

This document is a literal, step-by-step walkthrough, using 5 real published papers as worked examples. No botany or evolutionary biology background is assumed. You only need to be able to read a title and abstract.

NoteWhat a “tag dimension” means

A dimension is a category you’ll always want to filter by — like a column in a spreadsheet. A tag is one specific value inside that column. “Organism” is a dimension; formosensis is a tag inside it. You will derive both directly from real papers in Action 2 below — you do not need to invent them from scratch.

The 5 worked-example papers

These are real, verifiable papers relevant to your project. You can substitute your own papers later, but use these now so you have concrete material instead of a blank page.

# Paper Source
1 Su, Hsieh & Tsou (2009). “The confirmation of Camellia formosensis (Theaceae) as an independent species based on DNA sequence analyses.” Botanical Studies 50(4):477–485. PDF
2 Wu et al. “The Plastome of Camellia formosensis (Theaceae) Reconfirms the Endemism of Taiwanese Wild Tea.” TFRI
3 “Development of STS and CAPS markers for variety identification and genetic diversity analysis of tea germplasm in Taiwan.” Botanical Studies 55:12. Springer
4 Study on “Influence of natural light intensities on growth and content of catechins and caffeine in Camellia formosensis leaves and tea infusion.” (ResearchGate listing)
5 International Camellia Register species entry for Camellia formosensis, including TTES cultivar lineage notes (TTES #18 “Ruby,” TTES #24). Register
ImportantEvidence check before you proceed

Paper 1 is the taxonomic foundation of your whole project: it’s the study that established C. formosensis as its own species using nuclear DNA sequence analysis (the RPB2 gene), separate from C. sinensis var. sinensis and var. assamica. Everything downstream (TRES breeding, TTES cultivars) assumes this species boundary. If you only read one paper before building your taxonomy, read this one’s abstract.

Action 1 — Gather the 5 papers (or your own equivalents)

What to do: Open each link above (or search Google Scholar / NCBI / GBIF for your own 5–10 papers if you prefer). You only need the title + abstract for each — do not read the full paper yet.

Search keywords by topic direction

If you’re sourcing your own papers instead of using the 5 above, here are concrete search strings to use per topic area — these map directly to the dimensions you’ll build in Action 3, so searching across all four directions now gives you a more balanced starting table.

Taxonomy / species identity: - "Camellia formosensis" taxonomy - "Camellia formosensis" DNA sequence species - Camellia sinensis var. assamica vs sinensis Taiwan

Genomics / molecular: - "Camellia formosensis" plastome OR chloroplast genome - Camellia formosensis genome sequencing - tea plant Camellia genome NCBI

Lineage / breeding / cultivars: - TTES cultivar Taiwan tea breeding - "Taiwan Tea Research and Extension Station" cultivar - Qingxin Oolong cultivar lineage - STS CAPS markers tea germplasm Taiwan

Chemistry / phytochemistry: - Camellia formosensis catechin caffeine content - Taiwan wild tea phytochemical composition

Cultivation / agronomy / geography: - Camellia formosensis distribution Taiwan elevation - Taiwan tea growing region GBIF occurrence

TipWhy search across all four directions, not just one

If you only search “Camellia formosensis genome,” you’ll end up with 8 genomics papers and nothing on lineage or chemistry — your Action 2 table will look lopsided, and you won’t get a representative tag list. Pull 1–2 results from each direction above instead of maximizing results from one search.

Time: 10–15 minutes.

Watch out for: The temptation to start deeply reading paper 1 because it’s interesting. Resist — this stage is inventory, not comprehension. You’re scanning, not studying.

Action 2 — Fill in a 4-column table, one row per paper

For each paper, answer four questions in your own plain words — no jargon required, and nothing here requires you to evaluate the science:

  1. Organism — which species or cultivar is this paper about?
  2. Method — what kind of evidence did they generate (DNA sequencing, plant measurements, chemical analysis, historical records)?
  3. Pathway/topic — what relationship does this paper inform (is it about ancestry/lineage, where it grows, its chemistry, or its species classification)?
  4. Notes — anything that doesn’t fit cleanly (flag it, don’t force it)

Worked example, filled in from the 5 papers above

5. TipWhy this table is the actual taxonomy-building step

You didn’t invent “Taxonomy,” “Lineage,” and “Chemistry” as abstract categories — you read them directly off 5 real papers. This is the core trick: the categories already exist in your sources; your job is to notice and name them, not invent them.

Time: 20–25 minutes for 5 papers (less once you’re warmed up).

Action 3 — Turn each column into a tag list

Look down each column. Write the distinct, deduplicated values as a list. This is mechanical — you’re just removing repeats.

From the Organism column:

formosensis
sinensis-assamica
ttes-cultivar       (generic — use only when the specific cultivar/TTES number isn't named)
ttes-18             (specific cultivar, when named — "Ruby")
ttes-24             (specific cultivar, when named)
multi               (paper compares more than one organism, e.g. paper 1)
WarningWatch out for: synonym collision

Paper 5 names cultivars as “TTES #18” and “Ruby.” These are the same plant. Decide now: tags use the TTES number, not the common name, because TTES numbers are the canonical registry identifiers TRES itself uses — common names like “Ruby” or “Hong Yu” can vary by source. Keep a separate note (e.g. _synonyms.md) mapping common names to TTES numbers; don’t let the tag system absorb every synonym directly.

From the Method column:

genomics            (DNA/plastome sequencing — papers 1, 2)
genetic-markers      (STS/CAPS marker development — paper 3)
phytochemistry       (catechin/caffeine content — paper 4)
historical-record    (registry/bulletin entries, not primary research — paper 5)

From the Pathway/topic column:

taxonomy     (species classification — papers 1, 2)
lineage      (breeding/ancestry — papers 3, 5)
chemistry    (phytochemical profile — paper 4)
cultivation  (growing region/agronomy — not yet seen in these 5, but reserve it; you will hit it in TRES agronomy bulletins)

Time: 10 minutes. This step is just deduplication — no new judgment calls.

Action 4 — Write the taxonomy into one Obsidian note

Create a note called _taxonomy.md and pin/star it. Use Obsidian’s nested tag syntax (#dimension/value) — this renders hierarchically in the tag pane and lets you query either the parent (#organism) or a specific child (#organism/ttes-24) with Dataview.

# Tagging Taxonomy
*Last reviewed: <date>*

## #organism
- formosensis — wild Taiwanese tea species (Su et al. 2009)
- sinensis-assamica — introduced Assamica lineage
- ttes-18 — named cultivar "Ruby" (TRES registry)
- ttes-24 — named cultivar bred from wild formosensis populations
- ttes-cultivar — generic, use only if specific TTES number is unknown
- multi — paper covers more than one organism

## #method
- genomics — DNA/plastome sequencing
- genetic-markers — STS/CAPS or similar marker development
- phytochemistry — chemical composition analysis
- historical-record — registry entries, TRES bulletins, non-primary-research sources

## #pathway
- taxonomy — species classification questions
- lineage — breeding/ancestry relationships
- chemistry — phytochemical profile
- cultivation — growing region, agronomic practice

## Open/uncertain
- (papers that don't fit — review monthly, see Action 5 rule)

Time: 10 minutes to write this up cleanly from Action 3’s lists.

Action 5 — Set your rule for new/uncertain values now

You will hit a 6th paper that doesn’t fit any value above. Decide the rule before it happens, so you don’t have to think mid-reading-session:

  • Rule: Tag it #organism/unclear (or the equivalent for whichever dimension doesn’t fit) and move on immediately.
  • Batch review: Once a month, look at everything tagged unclear and decide then whether it needs a new permanent tag value or was a one-off.
ImportantWhy batching beats real-time decisions

If you stop to deliberate “is this a new tag or not?” every time you hit an edge case, tagging stops being a 2-minute task per paper and becomes a research project in itself. Given your ~3 hr/week budget, protect your reading time — defer the taxonomy edits.

Action 6 — Tag the 5 papers, and only the 5 papers, right now

Go into Zotero/Obsidian and apply tags to these 5 papers using only the values written in _taxonomy.md. Example for paper 5 (Camellia Register entry):

#organism/ttes-24 #organism/formosensis #method/historical-record #pathway/lineage

This is also your taxonomy’s first real test. If, by paper 5, you already want to add a tag that’s not on the list, that’s useful signal — but don’t add it live; note it and move to Action 5’s batch-review rule.

Time: 5 minutes for 5 papers.

What you should have at the end (checklist)

Total time for this whole document, start to finish: ~60–75 minutes, in one sitting or split across two sessions.

The end-to-end workflow: Zotero → Obsidian → Claude assist → tags

Once the taxonomy exists, here is the repeatable loop you’ll run for every new paper. This assumes your existing setup: Zotero (Better BibTeX + Zotero Integration plugin) and Obsidian (Dataview, Templater).

1. Zotero        →  2. Obsidian literature note  →  3. Claude assist  →  4. Tag + file
   (capture)          (auto-generated via plugin)      (summarize/extract)   (apply taxonomy)

Step 1 — Capture the paper in Zotero

Add the PDF/citation to Zotero as you normally would (browser connector, manual entry, or DOI lookup). This is your system of record for the citation itself — Obsidian should never hold the canonical bibliographic data, only notes about the paper.

Step 2 — Generate the literature note in Obsidian

Use the Zotero Integration plugin to pull the paper into Obsidian as a new note, using your Templater template (the one with fields for organism, gene IDs, dataset accession numbers — the bioinformatics-tuned template you’re building). This gives you a note with the citation metadata already filled in, and empty fields waiting for content.

Step 3 — Claude assist, inside that same note

This is where Claude does extraction/summarization work — but with two hard rules, given your principle that AI output must stay clearly distinct from your own analysis:

  • Label everything Claude produces. Use a literal heading like ## AI-Assisted Summary or an inline tag like #ai-generated, so six months from now you can tell at a glance what’s Claude’s extraction versus your own reading notes.
  • Claude extracts facts, you make judgments. Ask Claude to pull out things that are explicitly stated in the abstract/methods — organism, method type, gene IDs, accession numbers, sample size — not to infer significance or relevance. That interpretive layer is yours.

Concrete prompt pattern to use inside the Obsidian note (paste the abstract, then ask):

“From this abstract only, extract: (1) organism studied, (2) method type (genomics/morphology/field-survey/historical-record/etc.), (3) any gene IDs or accession numbers mentioned, (4) one sentence stating the main finding. Do not infer anything not explicitly stated.”

This keeps Claude’s output narrow, factual, and auditable — which matters if this note ever ends up referenced in a BISS manuscript.

WarningWatch out for: letting Claude pick the tags

Don’t ask Claude to “tag this paper” freely — it doesn’t know your controlled vocabulary and will invent plausible-sounding tags that don’t match _taxonomy.md. Instead, ask Claude to extract the four facts above, and you map them to tag values yourself (or ask Claude to pick only from a pasted list of your existing tag values — never an open-ended ask).

Step 4 — Apply tags from _taxonomy.md and file the note

Using the facts Claude extracted in Step 3, apply tags manually:

#organism/formosensis #method/genomics #pathway/taxonomy #ai-generated

If a value doesn’t exist yet in your taxonomy, follow the Action 5 rule from earlier in this document — tag unclear and batch-review monthly, rather than deciding live.

Why this division of labor works

  • Zotero owns citations (stable, structured, never touched by AI)
  • Obsidian owns your synthesis and the taxonomy (your judgment, your tags)
  • Claude owns narrow, auditable extraction (clearly labeled, never freely tagging or interpreting)

This mirrors the same separation you’re already building into the data pipeline — extraction/normalization is mechanical and reviewable, interpretation is a separate, deliberate step. The literature workflow is a smaller-scale dry run of the same discipline.

Objective of this phase, stated plainly

This entire document covers one phase of the tea heritage knowledge graph project: building the knowledge management layer — the taxonomy, synonym mapping, and literature workflow that everything downstream depends on.

What this phase is for: giving every paper you read a consistent, retrievable identity (which organism, what evidence type, what relationship) before any data gets extracted into DuckDB/Neo4j. The categories you define here (#organism, #method, #pathway) are not busywork — they are a dry run of the same boundaries your normalization layer will need (cultivar synonym resolution, entity types for nodes). Get them stable now, cheaply, in Obsidian; avoid re-deriving them under pressure later while also debugging a Cypher load script.

What this phase is explicitly NOT: data extraction, OCR pipeline work, schema design for Neo4j nodes/edges, or anything touching DuckDB. Those are the next phase. If you find yourself wanting to start the OCR pipeline or design Cultivar node properties while still tagging papers — that’s scope creep across phases. Finish this one first; the milestone below tells you when.

Milestone: Knowledge Management Layer Complete

You’re done with this phase — and clear to move to ETL/data extraction — when all of these are true:

What “complete” does NOT require

  • A perfect taxonomy. It will evolve — that’s what the monthly unclear review is for.
  • Every paper you’ll ever read tagged. 10–15 is enough signal; the rest gets tagged as you go, using the now-stable system.
  • Zero ambiguity. One or two unclear tags sitting in the batch-review queue is healthy, not a blocker.

Signal you’re NOT done yet

If you’re still renaming or restructuring dimensions (e.g., realizing “method” should actually split into two axes) past paper #10, the taxonomy hasn’t stabilized — finish that churn before starting ETL. Your DuckDB staging schema and normalization layer will lean on these same category boundaries; cultivar synonym resolution is literally the same problem as _synonyms.md, just at production scale.

Realistic timeline

Given a ~3 hr/week budget, this checklist is roughly 2–3 weeks of light, steady work — most of it is reading + tagging volume, not new decisions.

TipTracking progress without manual checking

Add a Dataview query to _taxonomy.md that counts tagged papers per dimension and surfaces anything still tagged unclear:

TABLE length(rows) as "Count"
FROM #organism OR #method OR #pathway
GROUP BY file.tags

And a dedicated query to flag your review queue:

LIST
FROM #organism/unclear OR #method/unclear OR #pathway/unclear

Check this monthly as part of the batch-review rule from Action 5 — it turns the milestone checklist above from something you track in your head into something Obsidian tracks for you.

What comes next

Once this taxonomy exists, the next step is the entity/relation schema for Neo4j — i.e., deciding exactly what properties a Cultivar node or Region node has. Notice that Action 3 above (organism tag list) and Action 4 (cultivar IDs vs. common names) are previews of that schema work — you are not duplicating effort, you’re front-loading decisions you need anyway.
