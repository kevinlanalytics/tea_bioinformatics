# Development of STS and CAPS markers for variety identification and genetic diversity analysis of tea germplasm in Taiwan

> **Authors:** Chih-Yi Hu, You-Zen Tsai, Shun-Fu Lin
> **Year:** 2014
> **Journal:** Botanical Studies
> **DOI:** 10.1186/1999-3110-55-12
> [Open in Zotero](zotero://select/library/items/TSGQZY3B)

## Abstract

Background: Tea (Camellia sinensis) is an important economic crop in Taiwan. Particularly, two major commercial types of tea (Paochong tea and Oolong tea) which are produced in Taiwan are famous around the world, and they must be manufactured with specific cultivars. Nevertheless, many elite cultivars have been illegally introduced to foreign countries. Because of the lower cost, large amount of “Taiwan-type tea” are produced and imported to Taiwan, causing a dramatic damage in the tea industry. It is very urgent to develop the stable, fast and reliable DNA markers for fingerprinting tea cultivars in Taiwan and protecting intellectual property rights for breeders. Furthermore, genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationship evaluations of tea germplasm in Taiwan are imperative for parental selection in the cross-breeding program and avoidance of genetic vulnerability.
Results: Two STS and 37 CAPS markers derived from cytoplasmic genome and ESTs of tea have been developed in this study providing a useful tool for distinguishing all investigated germplasm. For identifying 12 prevailing tea cultivars in Taiwan, five core markers, including each one of mitochondria and chloroplast, and three nuclear markers, were developed. Based on principal coordinate analysis and cluster analysis, 55 tea germplasm in Taiwan were divided into three groups: sinensis type (C. sinensis var. sinensis), assamica type (C. sinensis var. assamica) and Taiwan wild species (C. formosensis). The result of genetic diversity analysis revealed that both sinensis (0.44) and assamica (0.41) types had higher genetic diversity than wild species (0.25). The close genetic distance between the first (Chin-Shin-Oolong) and the third (Shy-Jih-Chuen) prevailing cultivars was found, and many recently released varieties are the descents of Chin-Shin-Oolong. This implies the potential risk of genetic vulnerability for tea cultivation in Taiwan.
Conclusions: We have successfully developed a tool for tea germplasm discrimination and genetic diversity analysis, as well as a set of core markers for effective identification of prevailing cultivars in Taiwan. According to the results of phylogenetic analysis on prevailing tea cultivars, it is necessary to broaden genetic diversity from wild species or plant introduction in future breeding programs.

## Key Claims Relevant to Tea KG

- 55 tea germplasm accessions analyzed (22 bred varieties from crosses, 9 local cultivars/landraces, 16 introduced varieties, 8 wild species) using 2 STS + 37 CAPS markers (10 cytoplasmic: 3 chloroplast C01–C03, 7 mitochondria M01–M07; 27 nuclear G01–G27; plus STS markers PAL and F3H), yielding 98 alleles across 39 polymorphic loci.
- Five "core markers" — M02 (mitochondria), C02 (chloroplast), and G01, G03, G04 (nuclear) — are sufficient to distinguish the 12 prevailing Taiwan cultivars (Chin-Shin-Oolong, TTES-12, Shy-Jih-Chuen, Chin-Shin-Dahpan, TTES-13, Chin-Shin-Gantzy, TTES-8, TTES-18, TTES-7, TTES-19, TTES-20, TTES-21). M02 splits sinensis vs. assamica type; G03/C02 split the assamica group into 4 cultivars; G03/G01/G04 split the sinensis group into 8 cultivars.
- PCoA and UPGMA cluster analysis (based on Modified Roger's Distance, MRD) divide the 55 germplasm into 3 groups: sinensis type (C. sinensis var. sinensis, S/SA), assamica type (C. sinensis var. assamica, A/AS), and Taiwan wild species (C. formosensis, F/FY). PC1 (24.5% variance) separates C. formosensis from cultivated C. sinensis; PC2 (15.9%) separates assamica from sinensis; PC1+PC2+PC3 cumulative = 51.8%.
- Average intra-group genetic distance (MRD): sinensis type (S/SA) = 0.44, assamica type (A/AS) = 0.41, wild species (F/FY) = 0.25 — i.e., cultivated types show higher internal genetic diversity than the wild species.
- Average inter-group genetic distance (Table 3): sinensis–assamica = 0.28; sinensis–wild species = 0.45; assamica–wild species = 0.47.
- Genetic distance across all 55 germplasm pairs ranged 0.08–0.69 (average 0.49); among the 47 cultivated (C. sinensis) accessions average = 0.47.
- Within sinensis type (37 accessions): distances ranged 0.11 (TTES-14 vs TTES-15) to 0.62 (Chin-Shin-Oolong vs TTES-17), average 0.44. Within assamica type (14 accessions): 0.08 (TTES-8 vs Jaipuri; Shan-1 vs Shan-2) to 0.58 (Shan vs Manipuri), average 0.41. Within wild species (8 accessions): 0.11 (Long-Tou vs Le-Ye wild tea; Ming-Hai vs Nan-Fong wild tea) to 0.38 (De-Hua-She vs Yung-Kang wild tea), average 0.25.
- Genetic diversity indices (nuclear markers, Table 4): Nei's gene diversity H = 0.36 (sinensis S/SA), 0.34 (assamica A/AS), 0.15 (wild F/FY); observed heterozygosity Ho = 0.38 (S/SA), 0.35 (A/AS), 0.16 (F/FY); Shannon's Information Index I = 0.58 (S/SA), 0.55 (A/AS), 0.26 (F/FY). Cultivated C. sinensis (combined S+SA+A+AS) has consistently higher genetic diversity than wild C. formosensis.
- Chin-Shin-Oolong and Shy-Jih-Chuen have a very close genetic distance of 0.21 (well below the 0.49 average), with all 10 cytoplasmic marker alleles identical between them — confirming Shy-Jih-Chuen is a genetic descendant of Chin-Shin-Oolong (consistent with earlier ISSR/leaf-morphology work by Hu 2004).
- Chin-Shin-Oolong is also recorded as the male parent of two more recent released varieties, TTES-19 and TTES-20 (both registered 2004).
- Together Chin-Shin-Oolong and Shy-Jih-Chuen account for 70.5% of all tea plantation acreage in Taiwan. Top three prevailing cultivars: Chin-Shin-Oolong (57.3%), TTES-12 (13.7%), Shy-Jih-Chuen (13.2%) — >84.2% combined; top five (adding Chin-Shin-Dahpan and TTES-13) cover >98% of Taiwan's tea acreage (2011 Tea Research and Extension Station statistics).
- This close relatedness among the dominant cultivars, plus heavy reliance on a small number of varieties, is flagged as a genetic vulnerability risk for Taiwan's tea industry (paralleling Japan's Yabukita cultivar, which alone covers >80% of Japan's green tea acreage, with several other prevailing Japanese cultivars — Kanayamidori, Sayamakaori, Saemidori, Okumidori, Meiryoku — themselves selected from Yabukita).
- UPGMA dendrogram (Figure 3): at MRD coefficient 0.57, Group I (C. formosensis, F/FY) splits off from all cultivated germplasm; at 0.51, Group II (assamica, A/AS) and Group III (sinensis, S/SA) separate. Group III further splits into IIIa (Chin-Shin-Oolong, Shy-Jih-Chuen, Bair-Mau-Hour, Wuu-Yi, Horng-Shin-Dahpan and derived TTES-3, TTES-4, TTES-9), IIIb (Tiee-Guan-In, Hwang-Gan and derived TTES-10, TTES-12, TTES-14, TTES-15, TTES-19), and IIIc (Chin-Shin-Gantzy, Chin-Shin-Dahpan and derived TTES-1; Dah-Yeh-Oolong and derived TTES-2, TTES-11, TTES-16, TTES-17, TTES-20; Ying-Jy-Horng-Shin and derived TTES-13).
- Marker informativeness: of 39 total markers, average PIC = 0.32 (range 0.04–0.62). 10 cytoplasmic markers: average PIC 0.25 (mtDNA avg 0.29, cpDNA avg 0.18), avg 2 alleles/locus. 29 nuclear markers: average PIC 0.34 (range 0.04 [G16] to 0.62 [G22]), 2–7 alleles/locus (avg 2.7). 11 nuclear CAPS markers (G03, G12, G14, G15, G17, G18, G20, G22, G23, G26 and one more) show multi-allele patterns; the rest are bi-allelic.
- Compared to a prior EST-SSR study by the same group (Hu et al. 2011), EST-SSR markers had higher average alleles/locus (5.6) and higher PIC (0.62) than the STS/CAPS markers here, but STS/CAPS markers (<1 kb) are argued to be more robust to DNA degradation from tea processing (heat, fermentation, panning).
- Historical/breeding claim: four landrace cultivars — Chin-Shin-Oolong, Dah-Yeh-Oolong, Chin-Shin-Dahpan, Ying-Jy-Horng-Shin — were introduced from China during the Japanese occupation period (AD 1896–1945); together with Hwang-Gan and Horng-Shin-Dahpan (also prevailing at that time), these six landraces have been used as female parents for hybridization breeding since 1945.
- Taiwan's tea industry is stated to have begun in the Jiaqing era of the Qing/Ching Dynasty (AD 1796–1820), introduced from China (Fukien/Fujian and Guangdong origins for some landraces).
- Taxonomic claim: Camellia formosensis is treated as a distinct, monophyletic species independent from C. sinensis, based on RPB2 nuclear gene sequence data and morphology (glabrous ovaries and winter buds) per Su et al. (2007, 2009) — used as the taxonomic basis for this study's wild-species classification.
- Discussion explicitly contrasts findings with Lai et al. (2001), who used RAPD/ISSR and reported the opposite diversity ranking (native Taiwan wild tea highest diversity, then sinensis, then assamica). The authors argue this is explained because: (1) two of Lai et al.'s "wild tea" samples (Laitou, Shueijing) are actually Camellia furfuracea rather than C. formosensis (per Su 2007), inflating apparent wild-species diversity; and (2) all three assamica samples in Lai et al. (2001) originated from India and are not representative of Taiwan's assamica-type germplasm.
- Taiwan tea farm statistics (2012, Council of Agriculture): 14,091 hectares, producing 17,310 tons/year. Cultivated tea distribution: Nantou County 48.4%, Chiayi County 15.5%, New Taipei City 10.6%. Assamica-type tea occupies ~3.9% of Taiwan tea acreage (mainly Nantou County); sinensis type ~96.1% (widely distributed).
- Processing-suitability property claims (Table 1): Chin-Shin-Oolong, TTES-12, Shy-Jih-Chuen, Chin-Shin-Dahpan, TTES-13 suitable for both Paochong and Oolong tea; Chin-Shin-Gantzy suitable for green tea; TTES-7, TTES-8, TTES-18 suitable for black tea.

## Methods Used

- Plant material: 55 tea germplasm accessions sampled in Taiwan (22 bred crosses, 9 local landraces, 16 introduced varieties, 8 wild species), collected mainly from the germplasm garden at the Tea Research and Extension Station, Taoyuan County, plus 4 samples (Shan-1–Shan-4) from Tung Pang Black Tea Co. Ltd., Nantou County.
- DNA extraction: modified Doyle and Doyle (1990) method (as described in Hu et al. 2005), from buds and leaves.
- STS (sequence tagged site) marker design: 2 chloroplast + 7 mitochondria primer pairs designed from cytoplasmic genome (per Hu 2004); nuclear STS/CAPS primers designed from 54 EST-derived primer pairs (4 from Kaundun and Matsumoto 2003/2004; 50 designed from NCBI dbEST) using Primer3 software; primers pre-screened on 8 cultivars (TTES-8, TTES-12, TTES-13, TTES-18, TTES-19, TTES-20, Chin-Shin-Oolong, Shy-Jih-Chuen).
- CAPS (cleaved amplified polymorphic sequence) marker design: nuclear amplicons sequenced via ABI PRISM 3730 DNA Analyzer; sequence analysis with SeqMan Pro v7.1 (DNAStar) to screen for SNPs/InDels; SNP/InDel sites converted to CAPS assays using SNP2CAPS software; markers validated by PCR-RFLP with specific restriction endonucleases and 2% agarose gel electrophoresis (ethidium bromide staining).
- Final marker panel: 2 STS markers (PAL, F3H) and 37 CAPS markers (10 cytoplasmic: C01–C03 chloroplast, M01–M07 mitochondria; 27 nuclear: G01–G27), detecting 98 alleles across 39 polymorphic loci in 55 germplasm.
- Marker statistics: Polymorphism Information Content (PIC) calculated with PowerMarker v3.25 (Liu and Muse 2005).
- Genetic diversity analysis: Popgene v1.32 (Yeh and Boyle 1997) used to estimate observed number of alleles (NA), effective number of alleles (Ne), observed heterozygosity (Ho), Nei's gene diversity (H), and Shannon's Information Index (I) per group (sinensis, assamica, wild species).
- Genetic distance / cluster analysis: pairwise genetic distances calculated via modified Roger's distance (MRD) method (Wright 1978) using TFPGA v1.3 (Miller 1997); UPGMA dendrogram constructed from MRD matrix; Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) performed with NTSYSpc v2.10 (Rohlf 1997), using first two extracted coordinates for the PCoA plot.
- Core-marker flowchart method: 5 of the 39 markers (M02, C02, G01, G03, G04) selected by variety-specificity and PIC value to build a hierarchical identification key for 12 prevailing Taiwan cultivars.

## Conflicts With Other Sources

- **Genetic diversity ranking directly contradicts [[laiAssessmentGeneticRelationships]] (Lai et al. 2001).** This paper (STS/CAPS markers) finds cultivated *C. sinensis* (sinensis H=0.36, assamica H=0.34) has *higher* genetic diversity than wild *C. formosensis* (H=0.15). Lai et al. (2001, RAPD/ISSR) found the opposite: native Taiwanese wild tea had the *highest* diversity of the three groups (Hs: RAPD 0.280, ISSR 0.253, vs. China tea 0.197/0.181 and Assam tea 0.074/0.114). This paper explicitly addresses the discrepancy in its Discussion, arguing two of Lai et al.'s "wild tea" samples (Laitou, Shueijing) were later shown by Su (2007) to actually be *Camellia furfuracea*, not true *C. formosensis*, inflating Lai et al.'s apparent wild-species diversity; and that Lai et al.'s Assam-type samples were all India-sourced rather than representative of Taiwan-grown assamica germplasm.
- **Taxonomic treatment of Taiwanese wild tea differs from [[laiAssessmentGeneticRelationships]].** This paper (2014) treats Taiwan wild tea as the independent species *Camellia formosensis*, per Su et al. (2007, 2009) — see [[suConfirmationCamelliaFormosensis]]. Lai et al. (2001) predates that reclassification and instead calls it *C. sinensis var. assamica f. formosensis* Kitam. (a form nested under the assamica variety). Same underlying taxon, different taxonomic rank/name — a pre- vs. post-2009 split that recurs across the vault (see also [[suConfirmationCamelliaFormosensis]]).
- **TTES No. 18 parentage ("H18; Burma × Taiwanese wild tea") corroborated by [[TTESNo18]]**, the official TBRS cultivar page, which agrees on parent identity/cross direction and additionally names the specific accession codes (B-729 maternal Burma parent, B-607 paternal wild-tea parent).
